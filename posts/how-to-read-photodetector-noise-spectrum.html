Photodetector noise measurements

How to Read a Photodetector Noise Spectrum

A measured spectrum can contain \(1/f\) noise, generation-recombination structure, Johnson noise, and the measurement system itself. The useful task is not merely naming three regions. It is determining which physical conclusions the data actually support.

Core idea: Noise mechanisms that are statistically independent add in power spectral density. The shape of the total spectrum can then be used to identify candidate mechanisms, but the shape alone rarely proves a unique microscopic cause.

At a glance

\[ S_V(f)=\frac{A}{f^\alpha}+\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}+C \]
  • Add uncorrelated contributions in power spectral density, not amplitude spectral density.
  • A low-frequency slope does not uniquely identify a particular trap or interface.
  • A Lorentzian corner gives a relaxation time only after electrical and instrument rolloffs are excluded.
  • A flat region is not automatically the detector's Johnson-noise limit.

Photodetector noise is commonly plotted as voltage power spectral density \(S_V(f)\) in \(\mathrm{V^2/Hz}\), or as voltage amplitude spectral density \(e_n(f)=\sqrt{S_V(f)}\) in \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). The two plots contain the same information, but slopes and fitted coefficients must be interpreted consistently.

A useful three-component model

A practical first model is

\[ S_V(f)=S_{1/f}(f)+S_{\mathrm{GR}}(f)+S_{\mathrm{white}} =\frac{A}{f^\alpha}+\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}+C . \]

The model is deliberately compact. It describes a low-frequency power law, one Lorentzian generation-recombination contribution, and a frequency-independent floor. Because the terms are power spectral densities, they may be added when the underlying fluctuations are uncorrelated.

!Do not fit this model mechanically. A real device may contain several Lorentzians, a broadened distribution of time constants, contact noise, bias drift, microphonics, amplifier noise, or a transfer-function rolloff. The three-term form is a starting hypothesis, not a guaranteed decomposition.

Reading the spectrum

Low frequency

The power-law term often dominates. Drift and environmental instability also become increasingly important as the measurement period grows.

Intermediate frequency

A GR plateau and corner may be visible when one relaxation process dominates and the measurement chain has adequate bandwidth.

High frequency

The spectrum may approach a white floor set by the detector, preamplifier, lock-in input, or a combination of them.

The \(1/f^\alpha\) region

\[ S_{1/f}(f)=\frac{A}{f^\alpha}. \]

The fitted exponent \(\alpha\) describes the observed slope. Values near unity are common, but \(\alpha\neq1\) is not unusual. A broad distribution of relaxation times can produce an approximate \(1/f^\alpha\) spectrum, but similar low-frequency excess noise may also be associated with contacts, surfaces, mobility fluctuations, carrier-number fluctuations, bias instability, or slow thermal drift.

The amplitude \(A\) is often more diagnostically useful when tracked against bias, current, resistance, area, temperature, processing history, and contact geometry. A spectrum by itself cannot identify which of those variables is responsible.

Practical interpretation: treat \(A\) and \(\alpha\) as measured descriptors first. Assign a microscopic mechanism only after checking how they scale with operating conditions and device geometry.

The generation-recombination region

\[ S_{\mathrm{GR}}(f)=\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}. \]

A single relaxation process produces a Lorentzian spectrum: a low-frequency plateau followed by a \(1/f^2\) rolloff in power spectral density. The corner frequency is related to the effective relaxation time by

\[ \tau_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2\pi f_c}. \]

This relation is valuable, but the word effective matters. Multiple recombination centers may generate multiple Lorentzians or a broadened feature. In HgCdTe photoconductors, deep levels can alter both recombination and carrier transport, so the measured corner need not represent one uncomplicated bulk minority-carrier lifetime.

The white-noise floor

For a resistor in the classical frequency regime, the Johnson-Nyquist voltage-noise density is

\[ e_{n,J}=\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}, \qquad S_{V,J}=4k_{\mathrm B}TR. \]

A flat measured spectrum should be compared with this prediction using the detector's actual resistance and temperature. The comparison must also include the preamplifier voltage noise, preamplifier current noise acting through the source impedance, lock-in input noise, and any gain used to refer the result back to the detector.

\[ e_{n,\mathrm{meas}}^2 = e_{n,\mathrm{det}}^2 + e_{n,\mathrm{preamp}}^2 + e_{n,\mathrm{instrument}}^2 +\cdots \]

A flat region above \(\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}\) does not disprove Johnson noise. It shows that the complete system has not yet been reduced to the detector's thermal floor.

When a noise corner is not a lifetime

The equation \(\tau=1/(2\pi f_c)\) is correct for a first-order relaxation. The difficult part is proving that the observed corner belongs to that relaxation.

!Before calling \(f_c\) a carrier-lifetime corner, exclude:

  • detector resistance with cable, contact, or input capacitance;
  • preamplifier bandwidth and compensation;
  • lock-in output filtering and ENBW conventions;
  • optical chopper, source, or readout transfer functions;
  • several overlapping trap or recombination time constants;
  • bias heating and operating-point drift.

The strongest interpretation comes from agreement among independent measurements: the noise corner, modulated responsivity rolloff, and time-domain rise or decay should produce compatible time constants under the same operating conditions.

A practical fitting workflow

  1. Plot PSD and ASD correctly. Confirm whether the data are in \(\mathrm{V^2/Hz}\) or \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), and verify that ENBW normalization has been applied exactly once.
  2. Establish the system floor. Measure a shorted input, open input, and known resistor through the same acquisition chain.
  3. Fit the white floor first. Estimate \(C\) only over a region that is flat and below any known measurement rolloff.
  4. Look for Lorentzian structure. Fit \(B\) and \(f_c\), then inspect residuals for additional corners or broadening.
  5. Fit the low-frequency term last. Estimate \(A\) and \(\alpha\) after accounting for the flatter components.
  6. Repeat across operating conditions. Bias, temperature, detector resistance, and device area provide the scaling information needed for physical interpretation.
  7. Validate in the time domain. Inspect drift, random-telegraph events, intermittent contacts, and nonstationarity that a smooth PSD can hide.

What the fitted parameters mean

ParameterDirect meaningWhat it does not prove by itself
\(A\)Amplitude of the fitted low-frequency power-law contributionA unique trap density or contact mechanism
\(\alpha\)Log-log slope magnitude of the power-law termThat one specific microscopic model is correct
\(B\)Low-frequency plateau of the fitted Lorentzian contributionWhich recombination center produced it
\(f_c\)Corner frequency of the fitted relaxationBulk minority-carrier lifetime without transfer-function checks
\(C\)Measured white PSD floorDetector Johnson noise unless the system floor is accounted for

Common interpretation mistakes

  • Adding amplitude spectral densities directly instead of adding uncorrelated powers.
  • Calling every low-frequency increase “trap noise” without checking drift, contacts, or environmental effects.
  • Assuming the three regions must appear as clean, nonoverlapping bands.
  • Extracting a lifetime from the first visible rolloff without de-embedding the electrical and instrument response.
  • Calling a flat region Johnson noise without comparing it with \(4k_{\mathrm B}TR\).
  • Fitting a stationary model to a record containing random-telegraph switching or slow drift.

What to report

A reproducible noise-spectrum result should include detector temperature, resistance, bias, device dimensions, optical condition, preamplifier and gain, acquisition method, sample rate, record length, windowing or averaging, ENBW, and the instrument-floor measurements. Report fitted parameter uncertainties and the frequency interval used for each fit.

The final result should distinguish three levels of confidence:

  • Observed: the measured slope, plateau, corner, or floor.
  • Modeled: the mathematical component used to fit that feature.
  • Interpreted: the physical mechanism proposed after independent checks.

Related reference: equivalent noise bandwidth

Before converting a filtered RMS value into spectral density, verify the exact ENBW and reporting convention of the instrument and filter mode.
