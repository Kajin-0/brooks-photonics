HgCdTe noise measurements

How to Read an HgCdTe (MCT) Noise Spectrum

A practical MCT noise sweep often reveals a low-frequency \(1/f\) region, a generation-recombination plateau and corner, and a high-frequency Johnson region. The measurement chain can imitate or obscure every one of them.

Core idea: An ideal MCT spectrum can be organized into \(1/f\), generation-recombination, and Johnson contributions. Independent mechanisms add in power spectral density; the measured amplitude spectral density is the square root of that sum.

At a glance

\[ \begin{aligned} S_V(f)&=\frac{A}{f^\alpha}+\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}+4k_{\mathrm B}TR,\\[4pt] e_n(f)&=\sqrt{S_V(f)} \end{aligned} \]
  • The \(1/f^\alpha\) term controls the low-frequency rise.
  • The GR term creates a plateau and a corner at \(f_c\).
  • The ideal high-frequency MCT floor is the Johnson term \(\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}\).
  • Preamplifier and instrument noise can lift the measured floor above the detector's Johnson level.

A noise spectrum does not divide into three perfectly isolated bands. The contributions overlap, and the apparent boundaries move with detector resistance, temperature, bias, geometry, material quality, contacts, passivation, and the measurement chain. The regions are therefore useful interpretive limits—not rigid material constants.

Power spectral density versus amplitude spectral density

MCT noise is commonly plotted as voltage power spectral density \(S_V(f)\) in \(\mathrm{V^2/Hz}\), or as voltage amplitude spectral density \(e_n(f)=\sqrt{S_V(f)}\) in \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\). A lock-in frequency sweep or spectrum analyzer commonly presents amplitude spectral density.

\[ e_n(f)=\sqrt{S_V(f)} \]

The distinction matters when reading slopes. If \(S_V\propto 1/f^\alpha\), then \(e_n\propto 1/f^{\alpha/2}\). A slope quoted from an ASD plot should not be inserted into a PSD model without that conversion.

!Add independent noise sources in power, not amplitude. Component amplitude spectral densities combine in quadrature. Adding the visible curve heights directly generally overestimates the total.

A useful MCT three-region model

A compact first model for the detector contribution is

\[ S_V(f)=S_{1/f}(f)+S_{\mathrm{GR}}(f)+S_J =\frac{A}{f^\alpha}+\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}+4k_{\mathrm B}TR. \]

The first term describes low-frequency excess noise. The second is a single Lorentzian relaxation with corner frequency \(f_c\). The third is the ideal Johnson-Nyquist PSD of the MCT resistance at temperature \(T\). This is an interpretive model, not a guarantee that every measured spectrum contains exactly one of each contribution.

!Do not fit the three terms mechanically. A real MCT device may contain multiple Lorentzians, broadened relaxation-time distributions, contact noise, current crowding, passivation-related fluctuations, bias heating, microphonics, preamplifier noise, or transfer-function rolloffs.

Reading the spectrum

1/f region

Low-frequency excess noise in MCT can reflect traps, surfaces and passivation, contacts, current nonuniformity, bias heating, or slow drift. The fitted slope is descriptive before it is microscopic.

GR region

A plateau and corner may appear when one effective recombination time dominates. Auger-1, Auger-7, SRH processes, and deep levels can all influence the observed MCT response.

Johnson region

After excess and GR noise roll off, the ideal detector floor approaches \(\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}\). The measured floor may remain higher because of the readout chain.

The \(1/f^\alpha\) region

\[ S_{1/f}(f)=\frac{A}{f^\alpha}. \]

The exponent \(\alpha\) describes the PSD slope. Values near unity are common, but departures from unity are not unusual. In MCT photoconductors, a broad distribution of trap and relaxation times can produce approximate \(1/f^\alpha\) behavior. Contacts, surfaces, passivation, carrier-number fluctuations, mobility fluctuations, nonuniform current flow, bias instability, and slow thermal drift can produce similar low-frequency excess.

The amplitude \(A\) becomes more physically useful when tracked against bias, current, resistance, area, temperature, contact geometry, processing history, and passivation state. One spectrum at one operating point rarely identifies a unique cause.

Practical interpretation: treat \(A\) and \(\alpha\) as measured descriptors first. Assign a microscopic mechanism only after their scaling with operating condition and device geometry has been tested.

The generation-recombination region

\[ S_{\mathrm{GR}}(f)=\frac{B}{1+\left(f/f_c\right)^2}. \]

A single relaxation process gives a Lorentzian PSD: a low-frequency plateau followed by a \(1/f^2\) rolloff. The corresponding amplitude spectral density rolls off as \(1/f\). For a genuine first-order relaxation,

\[ \tau_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{1}{2\pi f_c}. \]

The word effective matters. In HgCdTe, Auger-1, Auger-7, Shockley-Read-Hall recombination, deep levels, and transport effects can contribute overlapping time scales. Several processes can produce multiple Lorentzians or one broadened feature, so the observed corner need not equal one uncomplicated bulk minority-carrier lifetime.

The Johnson region

In the ideal MCT spectrum, the high-frequency detector floor is Johnson-Nyquist noise from thermal carrier motion through the detector resistance:

\[ e_{n,J}=\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}, \qquad S_{V,J}=4k_{\mathrm B}TR. \]

The Johnson region is frequency independent over the classical measurement band. Its level changes with the detector's operating temperature and resistance, so both values must be recorded at the same bias point used for the noise sweep.

!A flat high-frequency floor is not automatically the detector's Johnson region. Preamplifier voltage noise, preamplifier current noise acting through the MCT impedance, lock-in input noise, cable capacitance, gain errors, and bandwidth limits can determine the observed plateau.

\[ e_{n,\mathrm{meas}}^2 = 4k_{\mathrm B}TR +e_{n,\mathrm{preamp}}^2 +e_{n,\mathrm{instrument}}^2 +\cdots \]

A useful Johnson-region check compares the measured high-frequency floor with \(\sqrt{4k_{\mathrm B}TR}\), then repeats the acquisition with a known resistor and a shorted input through the same readout chain.

When a noise corner is not a lifetime

The equation \(\tau=1/(2\pi f_c)\) is correct for a first-order relaxation. The difficult part is proving that the measured corner belongs to that relaxation.

!Before calling \(f_c\) a carrier-lifetime corner, exclude:

  • MCT resistance with cable, contact, or input capacitance;
  • preamplifier bandwidth and compensation;
  • lock-in output filtering and ENBW conventions;
  • optical chopper, source, or readout transfer functions;
  • several overlapping trap or recombination time constants;
  • bias heating and operating-point drift.

The strongest interpretation comes from agreement among independent measurements. The noise corner, modulated-responsivity rolloff, and time-domain rise or decay should produce compatible time constants under the same temperature, bias, and optical condition.

Why the 1–10 kHz range is often useful

A 1–10 kHz range is often practical for MCT noise testing because it can lie above the strongest low-frequency excess while remaining well below a typical GR rolloff. That makes the measured value more representative of the GR plateau rather than the \(1/f\) rise or the high-frequency Johnson region.

This range is not universal. A detector with a lower GR corner, different lifetime, different bias, or significant instrumentation limits may require another frequency window. The correct test frequency is established from the measured spectrum and responsivity transfer function—not from a fixed convention alone.

A practical fitting workflow

  1. Confirm the units. Determine whether the data are PSD in \(\mathrm{V^2/Hz}\) or ASD in \(\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\), and verify that ENBW normalization has been applied exactly once.
  2. Record the MCT operating point. Include temperature, resistance, bias, current, device dimensions, optical condition, and stabilization time.
  3. Establish the system floor. Measure a shorted input, open input, and known resistor through the same preamplifier, cabling, gain, and acquisition settings.
  4. Check the Johnson prediction. Calculate \(4k_{\mathrm B}TR\) from the measured detector resistance and temperature, then compare it with the high-frequency plateau.
  5. Fit Lorentzian structure. Estimate \(B\) and \(f_c\), then inspect residuals for additional corners or broadening.
  6. Fit the low-frequency term last. Estimate \(A\) and \(\alpha\) only after the GR and high-frequency contributions are constrained.
  7. Repeat across operating conditions. Bias, temperature, resistance, area, contact geometry, and passivation provide the scaling needed for physical interpretation.
  8. Inspect the time series. Random-telegraph switching, intermittent contacts, thermal drift, and microphonics can be hidden by a smooth averaged spectrum.

What the fitted quantities mean

Common interpretation mistakes

  • Adding amplitude spectral densities directly instead of adding independent noise powers.
  • Using a PSD slope and ASD slope as though they were the same exponent.
  • Calling every low-frequency increase “trap noise” without checking drift, contacts, passivation, or bias heating.
  • Assuming the three regions must appear as clean, nonoverlapping bands.
  • Extracting a lifetime from the first visible rolloff without de-embedding electrical and instrument response.
  • Calling a flat high-frequency region Johnson noise without comparing it with \(4k_{\mathrm B}TR\) and the measured system floor.
  • Fitting a stationary model to a record containing random-telegraph switching or slow drift.

What to report

A reproducible MCT noise-spectrum result should include detector temperature, resistance, bias, current, device dimensions, optical condition, preamplifier and gain, acquisition method, frequency points or sample rate, record length, averaging, ENBW, and system-floor measurements. Report fitted uncertainties and the frequency interval used for each fit.

The final interpretation should distinguish three levels of confidence:

  • Observed: the measured MCT slope, GR plateau, corner, or high-frequency floor.
  • Modeled: the mathematical component used to fit that feature.
  • Interpreted: the physical mechanism proposed after independent checks.
