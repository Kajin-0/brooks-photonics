How to Convert Lock-In Noise from Vrms to V/√Hz Using ENBW

A practical quick reference for converting filtered lock-in noise into voltage noise density, comparing common instruments, and avoiding double normalization.

July 28, 202612-minute read

A detector-noise reading reported only as Vrms is incomplete. Its magnitude depends on the bandwidth admitted by the measurement chain. Equivalent noise bandwidth, or ENBW, converts a filtered RMS noise measurement into amplitude spectral density.

The core conversion

en = Vn,rms / √ENBW

Vn,rms is the RMS fluctuation after the output filter. ENBW is the ideal rectangular bandwidth that would pass the same white-noise power as the real filter.

Reference frequency is not measurement bandwidth. A detector can be measured at a 1 kHz reference frequency while the output filter admits only 1.25 Hz of equivalent noise bandwidth.

What ENBW means

ENBW = ∫0∞ |H(f)|² df / |H(0)|²

The definition is universal. The numerical coefficient is set by the complete transfer function and the convention used for the time-constant or bandwidth control. ENBW is therefore filter-specific, not an arbitrary manufacturer setting.

ENBW is not the same as the −3 dB bandwidth. The −3 dB bandwidth describes attenuation of a coherent signal. ENBW describes integrated white-noise power.

Worked example

  • Reference frequency: 1 kHz
  • Time constant: 100 ms
  • Filter slope: 12 dB/octave
  • Measured X standard deviation: 12.0 nV RMS

For a two-pole cascaded-RC filter:

ENBW = 1 / (8T) = 1.25 Hz
en = 12.0 / √1.25 = 10.7 nV/√Hz

SR830 and MFLI

Different interfaces, but identical ENBW coefficients for the first four orders when the filter topology and time-constant convention are the same.

Why coefficients can differ

  • Different filter topologies: RC, Butterworth, Bessel, FIR, or moving average
  • Different definitions of the displayed time constant
  • Additional synchronous, sinc, or antialias filtering
  • One-sided versus two-sided spectral-density conventions
  • Analog component tolerances and calibration in legacy instruments

Use the documented transfer function for the instrument and filter mode actually used. Do not import an SR830 or MFLI coefficient merely because another instrument shows the same time constant and roll-off.

Legacy analog reference: PAR/EG&G 124A

The PAR124A lacks modern software-defined averaging and spectral-analysis features, but its simpler analog signal path can be highly effective for precision narrowband work. In practical HgCdTe testing, it may produce more transparent and repeatable results than an SR830 when detector noise is only tens of nanovolts per square-root hertz.

This is not a universal instrument ranking. The relevant comparison is detector-referred input noise, preamplifier gain, filter response, overload recovery, grounding, drift, and the estimator used to report noise.

Important caution for SR830 users

The SR830 built-in X Noise and Y Noise displays are based on a moving mean absolute deviation estimator, not a direct RMS standard deviation. The result is rescaled under a Gaussian-noise assumption and is already normalized by ENBW.

Do not divide the SR830 X Noise or Y Noise display by √ENBW. Divide only when starting from an independently calculated RMS fluctuation of filtered X or Y data.

For stationary Gaussian noise, the MAD conversion is valid. HgCdTe data, however, can contain 1/f drift, random-telegraph events, microphonics, line pickup, intermittent contact noise, and bias instability. Those effects can make a Gaussian MAD conversion less representative than direct time-series and PSD analysis.

The SR830 input floor can be the larger limitation. With approximately 6 nV/√Hz input noise at 1 kHz, a detector contributing 10 nV/√Hz is measured in quadrature as:

√[(10 nV/√Hz)² + (6 nV/√Hz)²] = 11.7 nV/√Hz

That is about 17% above the detector contribution alone. For precision work, record settled X and Y data, calculate standard deviation and PSD offline, and measure the instrument contribution with a short, open input, and known resistor under the same configuration.

Practical workflow

  1. Record detector temperature, bias, resistance, reference frequency, gain, time constant, filter order, and ENBW.
  2. Allow the detector and output filter to settle.
  3. Acquire X and Y time series; do not use mean R as the noise estimator.
  4. Inspect the histogram and PSD for drift, lines, and non-Gaussian events.
  5. Refer measured noise through the preamplifier gain.
  6. Divide by √ENBW only when starting from filtered RMS noise.
  7. Repeat with shorted, open, and resistor inputs to establish the measurement floor.

Minimum reporting set

  • Noise density in V/√Hz or A/√Hz
  • Reference or analysis frequency
  • Detector temperature, resistance, and bias
  • ENBW, time constant, and filter order
  • Preamplifier gain and input-referred noise
  • Dark or illuminated condition
  • Acquisition duration and averaging method
