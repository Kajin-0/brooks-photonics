Equivalent Noise Bandwidth (ENBW): Converting Lock-In Noise to V/√Hz

ENBW is the bridge between a filtered RMS noise reading and noise amplitude spectral density. This guide develops the concept first, then applies it to common lock-in amplifiers and infrared detector measurements.

July 28, 202610-minute read

Definition: Equivalent noise bandwidth is the width of an ideal rectangular filter that transmits the same white-noise power as the actual measurement filter.

A detector-noise result reported only in volts RMS is incomplete. The measured RMS value increases as the admitted bandwidth increases, even when the underlying detector noise density is unchanged. ENBW removes that bandwidth dependence.

The basic conversion

en=Vn,rmsENBW

Vn,rms is the standard deviation or RMS fluctuation of the settled, filtered output. ENBW is in hertz. The result is expressed in volts per square-root hertz.

Reference frequency is not measurement bandwidth. A lock-in can detect at 1 kHz while its output filter admits only 1.25 Hz of equivalent noise bandwidth.

What ENBW means graphically

White-noise power at the output of a filter is proportional to the area under its power response, |H(f)|². ENBW replaces the real response with a unit-height brick-wall filter having the same area.

ENBW=∫0∞|H(f)|2df|H(0)|2

ENBW is not the −3 dB bandwidth

The −3 dB bandwidth identifies one point on the response curve: where coherent-signal power has fallen by one half. ENBW integrates the entire power response. For common low-pass filters, ENBW is therefore larger than the −3 dB bandwidth.

Worked example

  • Reference frequency: 1 kHz
  • Time constant: 100 ms
  • Filter slope: 12 dB/octave
  • Measured X standard deviation: 12.0 nV RMS

For a two-pole cascaded-RC filter:

ENBW = 1/(8T) = 1.25 Hz
en = 12.0/√1.25 = 10.7 nV/√Hz

The 1 kHz reference and 1.25 Hz ENBW describe different parts of the measurement.

Why the conversion can differ between instruments

ENBW is fixed by the complete filter transfer function, not simply by the brand name. Instruments differ when they implement different RC, Butterworth, Bessel, FIR, moving-average, synchronous, or antialias filters, or when they define the displayed time constant differently.

Use the manual for the instrument and filter mode actually used. The same displayed time constant does not guarantee the same ENBW on two unrelated instruments.

Practical observations from infrared detector measurements

For HgCdTe detectors with noise densities on the order of 10–30 nV/√Hz, ENBW is only one part of the uncertainty. Instrument input noise, preamplifier gain, estimator behavior, drift, microphonics, line pickup, and random-telegraph events can be equally important.

Laboratory observation: a legacy analog lock-in can sometimes feel more transparent for narrowband detector-noise work because the user sees a filtered analog output without hidden statistical normalization. That practical advantage should not be confused with a universal claim that analog instruments are always more accurate.

PAR/EG&G 124A

The PAR124A lacks modern automated averaging and data acquisition, but it can be highly effective for stable, narrowband measurements when its own input noise and filter characteristics are known. Its ENBW must be taken from the PAR124A filter response rather than assumed from an SR830 or MFLI table.

SR830 noise-display warning

The front-panel number is already in V/√Hz

When the SR830 display is set to X Noise or Y Noise, the front panel shows a numerical value without appending a visible “/√Hz” suffix. It can therefore look like an ordinary voltage reading. The SR830 manual states that X and Y Noise are displayed in Volts/√Hz and that the output-filter ENBW is already included.

Do not divide the displayed X Noise or Y Noise value by √ENBW again. That would normalize the result twice.

The built-in SR830 noise result is also not a direct RMS standard deviation. It uses a moving mean absolute deviation, or MAD, and rescales it to estimate RMS noise under a Gaussian-noise assumption. This is reasonable for stationary Gaussian noise, but HgCdTe measurements can contain drift, random-telegraph noise, intermittent contacts, microphonics, and other non-Gaussian behavior.

For precision work, record the settled X or Y time series and compute the standard deviation and power spectral density offline. Inspect the time trace and distribution rather than relying only on one built-in scalar.

The SR830 input-noise specification is also material at the low end of typical HgCdTe measurements. Combining an uncorrelated 10 nV/√Hz detector contribution with a 6 nV/√Hz instrument contribution gives approximately 11.7 nV/√Hz, a 17% increase. At 30 nV/√Hz, the combined result is approximately 30.6 nV/√Hz, only about 2% higher.

Common ENBW interpretation errors

  • Confusing the lock-in reference frequency with the output noise bandwidth.
  • Using −3 dB bandwidth and ENBW as if they were identical.
  • Dividing a noise value already reported in V/√Hz by √ENBW a second time.
  • Applying one instrument's time-constant conversion to a different filter topology.
  • Using R = √(X² + Y²) as a zero-signal noise estimator; its mean is positively biased.
  • Ignoring preamplifier and lock-in input noise when referring the result back to the detector.

A reproducible workflow

  1. Record detector temperature, resistance, bias, reference frequency, preamplifier gain, lock-in input configuration, time constant, filter order, and ENBW.
  2. Allow both detector operating point and output filter to settle.
  3. Acquire X or Y versus time and calculate the standard deviation after removing the mean.
  4. Refer the measured noise through the preamplifier gain to the detector input.
  5. Divide by √ENBW only when starting from a filtered RMS fluctuation.
  6. Repeat with a shorted input, open input, and known resistor to identify instrument, pickup, and Johnson-noise limits.
  7. Save enough settled data that the bandwidth-time product is large compared with unity; ENBW × record duration ≥ 100 is a useful starting target for stationary Gaussian noise.

Minimum reporting information

  • Noise density in V/√Hz or A/√Hz
  • Reference or analysis frequency
  • Detector temperature, resistance, and bias
  • ENBW, time constant, filter order, and filter mode
  • Preamplifier gain and input-referred noise
  • Dark or illuminated condition
  • Acquisition duration and estimator used
