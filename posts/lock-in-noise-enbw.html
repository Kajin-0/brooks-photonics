Photodetector noise measurements

How to Convert Lock-In Noise from Vrms to V/√Hz Using ENBW

A lock-in amplifier reports noise in volts RMS over the bandwidth of its output filter. Equivalent noise bandwidth converts that reading into voltage noise density so measurements made with different filter settings can be compared directly.

Terence Fisher July 28, 2026 8-minute read

A detector noise measurement reported only as Vrms is incomplete. The value depends on the bandwidth admitted by the measurement system. Increasing the admitted bandwidth passes more independent noise fluctuations and increases the measured RMS voltage.

Noise amplitude spectral density removes that bandwidth dependence. It is normally reported in volts per square-root hertz (V/√Hz) or nanovolts per square-root hertz (nV/√Hz).

The conversion

en = Vn,rms / √ENBW

Here, Vn,rms is the RMS random voltage measured after the lock-in output filter, and ENBW is the equivalent noise bandwidth of that filter in hertz.

The reference frequency is not the measurement bandwidth. A detector may be measured at a 1 kHz reference frequency while the lock-in output filter admits only 1.25 Hz of equivalent noise bandwidth.

What equivalent noise bandwidth means

A real low-pass filter does not stop abruptly at one frequency. ENBW is the width of an ideal rectangular filter that would pass the same total white-noise power as the real filter.

Consequently, ENBW is generally not equal to the filter's −3 dB bandwidth. It depends on the complete filter transfer function, including time constant and filter order.

Worked lock-in example

Measurement settings

  • Reference frequency: 1 kHz
  • Time constant: 100 ms
  • Output-filter slope: 12 dB/octave
  • Measured standard deviation of X: 12.0 nV RMS

For the conventional two-pole Stanford Research Systems filter:

ENBW = 1 / (8T) = 1.25 Hz
en = 12.0 / √1.25 = 10.7 nV/√Hz

Reporting 12.0 nV RMS without the filter settings would prevent a valid comparison with another detector or another instrument configuration.

Common SRS ENBW values

For the conventional cascaded-RC output filters used in instruments such as the SR830, the following relations apply, where T is the selected time constant.

Filter slopeENBWENBW at T = 100 ms
6 dB/oct1/(4T)2.50 Hz
12 dB/oct1/(8T)1.25 Hz
18 dB/oct3/(32T)0.9375 Hz
24 dB/oct5/(64T)0.7813 Hz

These expressions are instrument- and filter-dependent. Use the manual for the instrument and filter mode actually used.

MFLI measurements

The Zurich Instruments MFLI allows the demodulator filter to be displayed or configured using time constant, −3 dB bandwidth, or noise-equivalent power bandwidth. For noise normalization, use the displayed BW NEP value directly.

Do not divide twice. If an instrument or software module already returns a quantity in V/√Hz, it has already been normalized to bandwidth.

A practical measurement workflow

  1. Block the detector from ambient and test-source radiation unless the objective is photon-noise measurement.
  2. Record detector temperature, bias, resistance, reference frequency, preamplifier gain, lock-in input configuration, time constant, filter order, and ENBW.
  3. Allow the output filter and detector operating point to settle before collecting data.
  4. Acquire the X and Y time series. Use the standard deviation of X or Y after removing the mean.
  5. Avoid using the mean of R as the noise estimator. Because R = √(X² + Y²), R remains positively biased even when the true mean signal is zero.
  6. Refer the measured RMS noise back through any preamplifier gain before comparing it with detector-level noise models.
  7. Divide the detector-referred RMS noise by √ENBW.
  8. Repeat the measurement with a shorted input, open input, and a known resistor to identify instrument, pickup, and Johnson-noise limits.

How long should the record be?

Filtered noise samples are correlated, so the number of displayed samples is not the number of statistically independent observations. A useful planning metric is the bandwidth-time product:

BT = ENBW × Trecord

As a practical target, BT ≥ 100 gives an approximate one-standard-deviation uncertainty near 5% in the estimated amplitude spectral density for stationary Gaussian noise. At an ENBW of 1.25 Hz, this corresponds to roughly 80 seconds of settled data. Longer records are required when drift, 1/f noise, or intermittent artifacts are present.

When the simple conversion is insufficient

The equation en = Vn,rms/√ENBW treats the noise density as approximately constant across the admitted bandwidth. That is usually reasonable for a sufficiently narrow measurement band inside a local white-noise or flat generation-recombination region.

The approximation becomes less reliable when the output filter spans a rapidly changing 1/f spectrum, a generation-recombination corner, a detector-response pole, line-frequency interference, or a narrow mechanical or electrical resonance. In those cases, the measured variance must be modeled as an integral:

V²n,rms = ∫ SV(f)|H(f)|² df

The full noise spectral density and the actual transfer function then matter; a single scalar ENBW cannot reconstruct the underlying spectrum.

What should be reported

  • Noise density in V/√Hz or A/√Hz
  • Reference or analysis frequency
  • Detector temperature, resistance, and bias
  • ENBW, time constant, and filter order
  • Preamplifier gain and input-referred noise
  • Dark or illuminated condition
  • Acquisition duration and averaging method
