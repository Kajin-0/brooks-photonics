Carrier Lifetime, Bandwidth, and Rise Time

For a first-order photodetector response, one time constant connects three commonly measured quantities: carrier lifetime, the \(-3\,\mathrm{dB}\) response bandwidth, and the 10-90% rise time.

August 27, 20268-minute read

Core idea: if excess carrier population follows one dominant first-order relaxation time \(\tau\), then the same pole governs the transient response and the small-signal frequency response. That lets \(\tau\), \(f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}\), and \(t_{10-90}\) be converted directly.

Three useful single-pole relations

\[ \boxed{ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}, \qquad t_{10-90}=\tau\ln 9\approx2.197\tau, \qquad t_{10-90}\approx\frac{0.350}{f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}} } \]

Scope: these are first-order relations. They are exact for the ideal one-pole model used below. A real detector may contain several recombination, transport, thermal, RC, preamplifier, and readout time constants.

Lifetime to bandwidth

\(f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}=1/(2\pi\tau)\)

A shorter effective relaxation time supports a wider intrinsic response bandwidth.

Lifetime to rise time

\(t_{10-90}=2.197\tau\)

The 10-90% step-response interval is a fixed multiple of the same time constant.

Bandwidth to rise time

\(t_{10-90}\approx0.350/f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}\)

This familiar one-pole identity follows by eliminating \(\tau\).

Start from the carrier-rate equation

Assume a spatially averaged excess minority-carrier concentration \(\Delta n(t)\), a constant generation rate \(G_0\) after illumination is switched on, and one effective recombination lifetime \(\tau\). The first-order rate equation is

\[ \frac{d\Delta n}{dt}=G_0-\frac{\Delta n}{\tau}. \]

The recombination term is linear in \(\Delta n\). This is the same mathematical structure as a first-order RC low-pass system: one state variable, one pole, and one characteristic time constant.

Solve the optical step response

For an initially unilluminated detector, \(\Delta n(0)=0\). Solving the differential equation after a step to constant generation gives

\[ \boxed{\Delta n(t)=G_0\tau\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right)}. \]

The steady-state excess carrier concentration is therefore \(\Delta n(\infty)=G_0\tau\). After the optical generation is switched off at \(t=t_0\), the carrier population decays from its value at that instant:

\[ \Delta n(t)=\Delta n(t_0)e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau},\qquad t\ge t_0. \]

If the illumination has been on long enough to reach steady state before switch-off, then \(\Delta n(t_0)\approx G_0\tau\).

The same \(\tau\) sets both rise and decay

Derive the 10-90% rise time

Normalize the rising response by its steady-state value:

\[ p\equiv\frac{\Delta n(t)}{G_0\tau}=1-e^{-t/\tau}. \]

Solving for the time required to reach any fractional level \(p\) gives

\[ t_p=-\tau\ln(1-p). \]

At 10% and 90%,

\[ t_{10}=-\tau\ln(0.9)=0.10536\tau, \qquad t_{90}=-\tau\ln(0.1)=2.30259\tau. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ t_{10-90}=t_{90}-t_{10} =\tau\ln 9 =2.19722\tau \approx2.20\tau }. \]

Derive the frequency response and \(-3\,\mathrm{dB}\) bandwidth

Now let the optical generation contain a small sinusoidal modulation around a steady operating point:

\[ G(t)=\bar G+\delta G\,e^{i\omega t}. \]

The corresponding small-signal carrier modulation \(\delta n\,e^{i\omega t}\) obeys

\[ i\omega\delta n=\delta G-\frac{\delta n}{\tau}. \]

Solving for the modulation transfer function gives

\[ \frac{\delta n(\omega)}{\delta G(\omega)} =\frac{\tau}{1+i\omega\tau}. \]

After normalizing to the zero-frequency response, the magnitude is

\[ |H(f)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(2\pi f\tau)^2}}. \]

The \(-3\,\mathrm{dB}\) point occurs when the response amplitude has fallen to \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of its low-frequency value:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}\tau)^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau} } \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \boxed{ \tau=\frac{1}{2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}} }. \]

!Response bandwidth is not ENBW. Here, \(f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}\) describes the detector's coherent small-signal response. Equivalent noise bandwidth integrates a measurement filter's power response and is a different quantity. See Equivalent Noise Bandwidth (ENBW).

Eliminate \(\tau\): rise time directly from bandwidth

Substitute \(\tau=1/(2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}})\) into \(t_{10-90}=\tau\ln9\):

\[ t_{10-90} =\frac{\ln9}{2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}} =\frac{0.3497}{f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}}. \]

For a first-order response, this is normally written

\[ \boxed{ t_{10-90}\approx\frac{0.35}{f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}} }. \]

When does measured bandwidth actually give carrier lifetime?

The equations above identify the time constant of the pole that dominates the measured response. Calling that pole the minority-carrier lifetime requires additional physical justification.

A real photodetector measurement can contain several low-pass mechanisms:

  • bulk or effective recombination lifetime,
  • carrier transit or diffusion time,
  • device resistance-capacitance time constants,
  • contact and package capacitance,
  • preamplifier or transimpedance-amplifier bandwidth,
  • lock-in, digitizer, or other readout filtering,
  • thermal time constants in bolometric or heating-dominated behavior.

If the detector and electronics each contribute a first-order pole, the measured magnitude becomes

\[ |H_{\mathrm{meas}}(f)| = \frac{1}{ \sqrt{ \left[1+(2\pi f\tau_d)^2\right] \left[1+(2\pi f\tau_e)^2\right] } }. \]

The measured \(-3\,\mathrm{dB}\) point is then lower than the bandwidth of either isolated pole when the two time constants are comparable. A one-pole fit can still return a useful effective time constant, but it should not automatically be labeled the microscopic carrier lifetime.

Practical interpretation: if the electrical/readout bandwidth is at least several times larger than the observed detector rolloff and the measured phase follows the same one-pole model, extracting \(\tau\approx1/(2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}})\) becomes much more defensible.

Connection to generation-recombination noise

A single generation-recombination relaxation process produces the same Lorentzian pole structure in the noise spectrum:

\[ e_{\mathrm{GR}}(f) = \frac{e_{\mathrm{GR},0}}{ \sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2} }, \qquad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}}. \]

That is why a GR-noise rolloff can sometimes be used to estimate an effective relaxation time. The caveat is the same: HgCdTe and other infrared detectors can contain multiple recombination channels, traps, transport effects, contacts, and readout poles. A visible Lorentzian corner is therefore evidence for a characteristic time scale, not automatically proof of one unique bulk lifetime.

For a broader treatment of the spectrum, see How to Read an HgCdTe (MCT) Noise Spectrum.

A practical extraction workflow

  1. Measure the readout transfer function independently. Verify that the preamplifier, TIA, cables, digitizer, and lock-in do not set the observed corner.
  2. Acquire either a time-domain optical step or a frequency sweep. A step gives \(t_{10-90}\); a modulation sweep gives \(f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}\) and phase.
  3. Fit amplitude and phase together when possible. A true first-order pole has \(|H|=[1+(2\pi f\tau)^2]^{-1/2}\) and phase \(\phi=-\tan^{-1}(2\pi f\tau)\).
  4. Cross-check the two domains. Compare \(\tau=t_{10-90}/2.197\) with \(\tau=1/(2\pi f_{-3\mathrm{dB}})\). Agreement supports the one-pole model.
  5. Use cautious terminology. Report \(\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}\) unless detector physics and de-embedding justify interpreting the pole as a specific carrier lifetime.

Reference equations

The derivation is based on the linear first-order carrier-rate equation and its standard step and sinusoidal steady-state solutions. The resulting identities are exact within that model:

\[ t_{10-90}=\tau\ln9, \qquad f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}, \qquad t_{10-90}f_{-3\mathrm{dB}}=\frac{\ln9}{2\pi}\approx0.3497. \]
