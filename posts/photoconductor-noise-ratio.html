Photoconductor noise screening

A Two-Frequency Noise Ratio for Screening Photoconductors

A pair of noise measurements cannot replace a full spectrum, but it can provide a fast and repeatable indication that low-frequency excess noise is extending into the normal detector test band.

Core idea: compare the noise amplitude spectral density at a lower frequency \(f_L\) with a higher reference frequency \(f_H\). The resulting ratio is a simple screening heuristic for low-frequency excess noise—not a microscopic noise model.

At a glance

\[r_{L/H}=\frac{e_n(f_L)}{e_n(f_H)}\]
  • \(r_{L/H}\approx1\): the measured noise is nearly flat between the two points.
  • \(r_{L/H}>1\): the lower-frequency point is elevated relative to the reference.
  • A larger ratio indicates a stronger low-frequency rise over that interval, but does not identify its cause.

Why two points can be useful

A full frequency sweep is the correct way to resolve the shape of a photoconductor noise spectrum. It reveals whether the detector exhibits a low-frequency rise, a comparatively flat generation-recombination region, a rolloff, and a higher-frequency floor.

Full sweeps, however, take more time than measurements at a small number of standard frequencies. In detector production, process development, or repeated laboratory characterization, the same two test frequencies may already be recorded for every device. Those two values contain a useful comparison that is often left unused.

The higher-frequency measurement can serve as a reference for the flatter portion of the spectrum. The lower-frequency measurement tests whether excess noise has begun to rise above that reference. The ratio does not tell the entire story, but it can identify which detectors require the full story.

The two-frequency noise ratio

Let \(e_n(f)\) denote noise amplitude spectral density, normally reported in \(\mathrm{V/\sqrt{Hz}}\) or \(\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\). Define

\[r_{L/H}=\frac{e_n(f_L)}{e_n(f_H)}, \qquad f_L

The ratio is dimensionless. It compares the shape of the measured spectrum over one selected interval rather than comparing only absolute noise levels.

For example, 1 kHz and 10 kHz are convenient values when both are part of an established detector test procedure and 10 kHz lies within a stable, flat measurement region. In that case,

\[r_{1/10}=\frac{e_n(1\,\mathrm{kHz})}{e_n(10\,\mathrm{kHz})}.\]

Nothing fundamental makes 1 kHz and 10 kHz universal. Their value comes from repeatability, familiarity, and their location within the useful test band for the detector population being measured.

How to choose \(f_L\) and \(f_H\)

The ratio is only useful when the two frequencies have stable and repeatable roles in the measurement.

The higher point \(f_H\) should lie in a verified flat region that is not yet affected by detector rolloff, cable capacitance, preamplifier bandwidth, lock-in response, or another high-frequency limitation. It acts as the reference.

The lower point \(f_L\) should lie below \(f_H\), but still within a frequency range where the measurement is stable and free from strong line harmonics, drift, or isolated interference. Its purpose is to test whether the low-frequency rise is beginning to intrude into the normal test band.

Why a one-decade pair is convenient: using the same frequency spacing for every detector makes the ratio easy to compare across devices, lots, biases, temperatures, and process changes. It does not require the spectrum to obey an exact \(1/f\) law.

How to interpret the ratio

\(r_{L/H}\approx1\)

The two measurements are nearly equal. This is consistent with a relatively flat spectrum over the selected interval, provided the detector—not the readout floor—is being resolved.

\(r_{L/H}>1\)

The lower-frequency point is elevated. Low-frequency excess noise may be extending into the test band, or another frequency-dependent contribution may be present.

\(r_{L/H}<1\)

The higher-frequency point is larger. This is a reason to check narrowband pickup, resonances, readout behavior, detector rolloff conventions, or measurement inconsistency.

A ratio of 2, for example, means only that the measured noise amplitude spectral density at \(f_L\) is twice the value at \(f_H\). It does not prove that the spectrum follows \(1/f\), identify a trap distribution, or establish a particular microscopic mechanism.

!Do not turn a two-point heuristic into a fitted noise law. Two frequencies cannot determine a reliable slope, resolve multiple Lorentzians, or distinguish detector noise from every possible measurement artifact.

Connection to the low-frequency knee

The low-frequency knee, or corner, is the transition between the rising excess-noise region and the flatter mid-frequency region. Its exact definition depends on the model and fitting convention, but its practical meaning is straightforward: it indicates how far the low-frequency contribution extends into the usable measurement band.

If the knee lies well below \(f_L\), both selected points may fall on the same flat region and the ratio should remain near unity. If the knee approaches or exceeds \(f_L\), the lower-frequency measurement may rise while \(f_H\) remains comparatively flat, increasing \(r_{L/H}\).

The ratio therefore gives a quick indication that the knee may be moving upward, but it does not locate the knee accurately. A full frequency sweep is required to determine the transition and test whether it is genuinely associated with low-frequency detector noise.

Why the ratio helps compare detectors

Absolute noise at one frequency is important, but it does not describe how rapidly the spectrum rises toward lower frequency. Two detectors can have similar noise at \(f_H\) and very different behavior at \(f_L\). Conversely, a detector with a higher absolute plateau may still have a flatter and more stable low-frequency spectrum.

A consistent ratio adds a second dimension to detector comparison:

  • Noise at \(f_H\): the absolute level in the selected flat reference region.
  • \(r_{L/H}\): the relative rise toward lower frequency.

Across a detector population, the ratio can be used to identify devices that differ from the normal distribution, even before a complete spectrum is acquired for every unit. It is most useful as a relative comparison within a controlled measurement program rather than as a universal pass/fail number.

What an elevated ratio may flag

A detector with unusually high low-frequency noise may deserve further investigation of:

  • passivation quality and surface or interface states;
  • contacts and contact-related fluctuations;
  • current crowding or nonuniform current flow;
  • material or process nonuniformity;
  • bias dependence and detector self-heating;
  • slow thermal drift or unstable operating conditions;
  • electromagnetic pickup or readout contamination.

These are candidate explanations, not conclusions. The ratio is valuable because it tells the engineer where to look next—not because it uniquely identifies the problem.

What the ratio cannot prove

The notation “\(1/f\) noise” is often used broadly for low-frequency excess noise, but a two-frequency measurement cannot verify that the spectrum follows an exact inverse-frequency law. It also cannot determine whether the source is a broad trap distribution, a contact process, a surface effect, thermal drift, random telegraph behavior, or the measurement chain.

The ratio should therefore be described as a low-frequency-noise screening heuristic. If it is elevated, follow with a full noise sweep and controlled changes in bias, temperature, geometry, passivation, or readout configuration.

When the ratio represents detector behavior

The two measurements must be made under equivalent conditions. Detector temperature, bias, resistance, optical condition, preamplifier gain, averaging, and equivalent noise bandwidth should remain controlled or be correctly normalized.

For biased photoconductors, battery-based bias can reduce power-supply ripple and switching contamination. An appropriately matched ultra-low-noise preamplifier helps keep the readout floor below the detector. A Faraday cage and disciplined grounding reduce the chance that electromagnetic pickup is mistaken for a detector-specific low-frequency rise.

These measures do not define the ratio. They determine whether the ratio belongs to the detector.

A compact reporting format

A useful detector-comparison record can report:

\[e_n(f_L),\qquad e_n(f_H),\qquad r_{L/H},\]

together with the detector temperature, bias, resistance, optical condition, ENBW, preamplifier, and averaging method. When 1 kHz and 10 kHz are used, report them explicitly rather than treating the frequencies as universal standards.

Final interpretation: a high \(r_{L/H}\) is not proof of a particular \(1/f\) mechanism. It is a fast, repeatable indication that low-frequency excess noise may be reaching farther into the detector’s normal test band than it does in comparable devices.

The full spectrum remains the final test

The two-frequency ratio is most valuable as a screening tool. Use it to decide which detectors, process conditions, or operating points require a complete noise-spectrum analysis.
